1

Introduction
In 2020, an estimated 595 million people worldwide were living with osteoarthritis, corresponding to approximately 7.6% of the global population (Steinmetz et al.  2023 ). With a prevalence of 365 million, osteoarthritis in the knee is the most affected joint (World Health Organization  2023 ). A common treatment for severe knee osteoarthritis is total knee arthroplasty (TKA), and the number of TKA procedures performed worldwide has continued to increase steadily in recent years with an estimated 120–200 procedures per 100,000 people in the United States and similarly high rates in many European countries (Kittelson et al.  2020 ). In Denmark, 14,878 primary knee arthroplasties and 1342 revision procedures were performed between April 1, 2023 and March 31, 2024, representing the highest annual volume recorded to date (Danish Kneealloplasty Register  2024 ).
Total knee arthroplasty is conducted primarily to relieve pain and reduce limitations in knee mobility (Myles et al.  2002 ). Therefore, range of motion (ROM) is an important clinical indicator of patient progress during postoperative rehabilitation (Ebert et al.  2014 ). A common goal in various postoperative protocols is to achieve full extension to at least 115°–120° of flexion after rehabilitation (T. Jakobsen and Mark‐Christensen  2021 ; The Ohio State University,  n.d. ; University Orthopedics and Adult Reconstruction Division,  n.d. ). Achieving approximately 110° of knee flexion allows individuals to perform routine activities of daily living (ADL), including sitting down and standing up from a low chair, level walking, ascending‐ and descending stairs, and bicycling, more effectively (Ercison et al.  1988 ; Rowe et al.  2000 ; Rowe et al.  2005 ).
People, undergoing TKA generally expect pain relief and improved ability to perform ADLs (Bourne et al.  2009 ; Itoh and Matsuda  2024 ; Tsuda and Matsuda  2024 ). However, a subgroup of patients fails to achieve the expected outcomes of surgery and rehabilitation, experiencing persistent pain, functional limitations, quadriceps weakness, and reduced mobility (M. Silva et al.  2003 ; Mizner et al.  2005 ; Devers et al.  2011 ; Beswick et al.  2012 ).
Studies show that recovery of flexion is nonlinear, with greatest improvements occurring during the first 4–6 weeks after surgery, followed by more gradual progress the following weeks (Ebert et al.  2014 ; Kornuijt et al.  2019 ; Kittelson et al.  2020 ). Insufficient recovery of ROM can result in persistent dysfunction and patient dissatisfaction due to limited ability to use the replaced joint and perform ADLs, factors closely associated with quality of life (Rowe et al.  2000 ; Rowe et al.  2005 ; Bourne et al.  2009 ; Matsuda et al.  2013 ). Furthermore, reduced mobility in the knee after TKA can lead to a prolonged and more painful postoperative course. Unsatisfactory surgical outcomes also entail economic costs, including reduced or lost work ability and extended sick leave (Maloney  2002 ). To support adequate postoperative ROM, therapeutic guidelines emphasize passive and active flexion exercises in both non‐weight‐bearing and weight‐bearing positions. However, various studies have demonstrated that continuous passive motion (CPM) has no significant effect on ROM (Harvey et al.  2010 ; Boese et al.  2014 ; Herbold et al.  2014 ; Joshi et al.  2015 ; Yang et al.  2019 ; Richter et al.  2022 ). While active flexion exercises are essential for achieving high ROM, only a limited number of randomized controlled trials have investigated mobility training after TKA (Mockford et al.  2008 ; Chow and Ng  2010 ; Papotto and Mills  2012 ). Additionally, the dose‐response relationship for active ROM exercises has not been explored. Because of limited evidence supporting guidelines following TKA, clinical practice commonly relies on best‐practice recommendations. Home‐based rehabilitation programs are widely used, and they may be beneficial as either standalone interventions or supplements to supervised therapy. However, evidence for the effectiveness of self‐managed exercise programs remains limited, and further research on adherence is needed. This is particularly relevant for individuals with insufficient ROM after TKA in whom optimized rehabilitation strategies are essential. Thus, this study investigated the effects of high‐ versus low‐frequency self‐managed knee flexion exercises in individuals with reduced knee flexion (≤ 105°), 6–26 weeks after TKA.

2

Methods

2.1

Study Design
The study was a randomized controlled trial conducted from October 2022 to March 2024. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Capital Region, Denmark (Prot. 22020545) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov ( NCT06188091 ). All procedures were carried out in accordance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and approved by the Danish Data Protection Agency (P‐2022‐756).
The study was conducted at the Department of Rehabilitation Montebello, Benalmadena, Spain, which is affiliated with the Region of Copenhagen under North Zealand Hospital as part of the Danish public healthcare system. The department receives referrals from physicians across Denmark, including hospital doctors, general practitioners, and private practice specialists.

2.2

Participants
The recruitment period was conducted from October 29, 2022 to March 25, 2023 and from October 21, 2023 to March 16, 2024. Recruitment was suspended from April to October 21, 2023 to help ensure comparable groups and to minimize seasonal effects including differences in weather conditions and available training. Consequently, participants were not recruited during the period in which pool‐based training was offered as a potential co‐intervention in the study.
The participants were recruited nationwide in Denmark. Inclusion criteria were adults (≥ 18 years), primary or revision TKA performed within 6–26 weeks prior to enrollment, and active knee flexion ≤ 105° on the operated side. Participants with unilateral arthroplasty or other surgical procedures involving the knee joint were excluded from the study.
All participants received oral and written information about the study, including procedures for data handling and the right to withdraw at any time. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion.
The randomization was carried out by an administrative employee after completion of the baseline assessments. This employee was affiliated with a separate department and had no contact with the participants during the intervention period. The study used cluster randomization, with clusters defined as rehabilitation groups commencing on the same day. All participants within each cluster received identical instructions regarding the frequency of the exercises. Cluster randomization was employed because of the impracticality of delivering different intervention protocols to participants within the same rehabilitation group and to minimize the risk of contamination between participants. Randomization was performed using the sealed envelope method. There were two envelopes more than required to reach the needed number of participants. The papers indicating group allocation (× 2 and × 8) were folded to conceal the group names and placed in opaque envelopes. The order of the envelopes was randomized prior to the allocation.

2.3

Intervention
The participants were allocated to perform flexion and extension exercises either eight times daily referred to as High Frequency (HF) group or two times daily referred to as the Low Frequency (LF) group. Both groups received instruction on the prescribed exercises from a physiotherapist on the first day of training, which was repeated on day three.
To improve flexion, the participants received instructions in two exercises which they could choose. (1) Standing on a staircase with the operated leg placed two steps up and holding the handrail for balance. The body moving forward until end‐range knee flexion was reached, while ensuring that the knee remained aligned with the foot on the same side (Figure  1a ). (2) Lying supine on a couch with the operated leg placed on a Böhler knee flexion frame, the knee supported on the cushion of the frame, and the ankle of the non‐operated leg positioned on the instep of the operated foot. Pressure was applied until end‐range flexion was reached (Figure  1b ). To improve extension, participants were instructed in two exercises from which they could choose. (1) Sitting on a couch with a rounded bolster or similar under the leg placed at the level of the Achilles tendon, the contralateral leg reaching the floor, and the arms extended posteriorly to support the upper body. The participants were instructed to actively press the knee into end‐range extension by activating the quadriceps muscle (Figure  1c ). (2) Sitting on a chair with the operated leg extended forward and the heel supported on the floor. The hands were placed above the patella using a fork grip, applying even pressure to achieve end‐range of knee extension (Figure  1d ).

FIGURE 1 Illustration of the four exercises to increase the range of motion. (a) Knee flexion on stairs; (b) knee flexion with Böhler; (c) knee extension with leg on a couch; (d) knee extension sitting on a chair.
All exercises were performed using 10 repetitions per session, with a pause of 3–5 s between each repetition, and the end‐range position was held for 10 s. Participants alternated between exercises, performing one flexion exercise followed by one extension exercise. In addition to the oral explanation, participants received written and illustrative material to support the exercises. Following instruction, the exercises were performed without supervision, and participants were provided with a logbook to record each exercise session. The logbook was reviewed daily by a physiotherapist. Participants were instructed that pain or tightness at end‐range during the exercises could occur and that temporary knee joint irritation might be experienced immediately after exercise. Any irritation was required to subside before the exercises were repeated. The intervention was conducted over 18 consecutive days during hospitalization.
In parallel with the specific flexion and extension exercises, all participants received a standardized rehabilitation program throughout the intervention period. The program comprised supervised group sessions led by a physiotherapist and focused on improving muscle strength, joint stability, movement coordination, and gait. The daily 45‐min exercise sessions varied in content and generally consisted of four components: a warm‐up (10 min), balance and coordination exercises performed in various positions (10 min), light resistance training targeting major muscle groups, with each exercise lasting 1.5 min followed by a 1‐minute rest intervals (20 min), and stretching and cool down activities (5 min). In addition, participants engaged in supervised gym‐based training sessions using exercise machines, such as stationary bicycles, rowing machines and cross‐trainers, for warm‐up and cardio training. Lower body strength training was performed using leg press, calf press, hip abductor and adductor machines, leg curl machines, and hip extension machines. Exercises were performed for 15–20 repetitions to volitional fatigue in three sets with 1‐minute rest intervals. Upper‐body exercises were performed using a back extension machine, lat pulldown, dual adjustable pulley and cable station. These exercises were likewise performed for 15–20 repetitions in three sets with 1‐minute rest intervals. Educational sessions were provided on knee surgery and postoperative precautions, basic training principles, and use of transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation for pain management. The co‐intervention (the standardized rehabilitation program) followed a structured written curriculum provided to all patients with TKA enrolled in the 18‐day rehabilitation program. The physiotherapists delivering the intervention were experienced and trained to provide treatment according to a standardized manual, specifying the day‐to‐day training and educational sessions.

2.4

Outcome
The primary outcome was the change in knee flexion from baseline to re‐test and the sample size calculation was based on this outcome. Prior to study initiation, all physiotherapists responsible for joint measurements received standardized training in the selected measurement procedure to ensure optimal measurement reliability. To further enhance validity and reliability, the same therapist preformed both the baseline and re‐test measurements using manual goniometric assessment (T. L. Jakobsen et al.  2010 ). Flexion was measured manually using a goniometer and the participant was positioned supine. Anatomical landmarks were identified at the most prominent aspect of the greater trochanter, lateral epicondyle of the femur, the head of the fibula and the lateral malleolus. The axis of the goniometer was aligned with the lateral epicondyle of the femur. Participants were instructed to actively flex the knee, drawing the heel as close to the gluteal region as possible. Extension was assessed with the participant in a supine position and a firm bolster placed beneath the heel of the test leg. The same anatomical landmarks were used, and the goniometer axis was again aligned with the lateral epicondyle of the femur. Active ROM was measured for all participants on the first day of training at 8:00 a.m., prior to randomization, and subsequently after 18 days of intervention (day 19 at 8 a.m.).
Measures to ensure assessor blinding and reduce the risk of bias were taken by choosing therapists working in clinical specialties unrelated to knee conditions such as neurology, rheumatology, or occupational therapy. The assessments were performed at a separate hospital location from where the daily training program was provided. The assessors did not interact with the participants during the 18‐day intervention period. Participants and outcome assessors were explicitly instructed not to communicate with each other about the group allocation. Furthermore, the group allocation of participants was not recorded in their medical records. The documentation only indicated that participants had received information about the study and had provided informed consent. Thus, the medical record did not reveal the allocation for any clinical employees at the hospital.
Secondary outcomes included knee extension and the Six‐Minute Walk Test (Six‐MWT; Rikli and Jones  2013 ). The participants walked 6 minutes, and the distance was registered in meters. The patients were allowed to use walking aids as needed. Physical function was tested using the 30 Second Chair‐Stand‐Test (30‐CST; Rikli and Jones  2013 ) where the participants were instructed to rise to a full standing position and return to a seated position as many times as possible within 30 s, while the arms remained folded across the chest. Pain was reported for the operated knee before and after the Six‐MWT using Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) ranging from 0 to 10 (higher score indicating more pain). Health symptoms and functionality of the knee were measured using the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS; Roos and Lohmander  2003 ), a questionnaire covering five subscales of pain, symptoms, ADL, sports/recreation, and quality of life. The subscales consist of multiple items scored on a 4‐point Likert scale. The subscale sports/recreation was not included in this study as it measures activities often contraindicated by the surgeons. Exclusion of sports/recreation is consistent with previous research (Skou et al.  2015 ; Larsen et al.  2020 ). The answers to questions regarding pain, symptoms, ADL, and knee‐related quality of life were scored according to standard procedure on a scale ranging from 0 (worst) to 100 (best). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using the participants' height and weight (kg/m 2 ).

2.5

Statistical Analysis
Sample size estimations were performed based on the assumption prior to the study that the LF group would increase flexion by four degrees, compared to the expectation that the HF group would increase flexion by 12°. Thus, there would be a difference of eight degrees between the two groups. Based on these assumptions, the sample size was calculated with an equal number of participants in the two groups (ratio 1:1), 80% power, and a significance level of 5%. The calculation resulted in 30 participants in each group. An additional 20% was added to account for dropout. Therefore, the minimum number of included patients in each group was 36.
The analysis of the effect on the primary outcome was an intention to treat analysis. As there were no missing data in the primary outcome, data imputation was not needed. The analyses of the secondary outcomes were conducted per protocol without data imputation, as the missing data was assumed to be missing at random. All outcome variables were inspected for normal distribution using Q–Q plots. The changes in outcomes were normally distributed and the differences between the two groups were analyzed using Student's unpaired t ‐test. The difference between the groups' changes the primary outcome was also tested in a sensitivity analysis using a linear regression model adjusted for baseline knee flexion. A test for multiple significance was performed using the False Discovery Rate in R Studio. Data are presented as numbers and percentages, mean and standard deviation, mean and [95% confidence interval (CI)], or median (interquartile range [IQR]). For correlation analysis of flexion at baseline and change in flexion and for dose‐response analyses Persons Correlation was used. p  &lt; 0.05 was significant. Analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS 25 program (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) or R Studio.

3

Results
During the enrollment period, 330 patients were hospitalized and assigned to rehabilitation after knee surgery or various injuries to the knee. A total of 245 patients were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. In total, 85 persons were enrolled in the study and randomly assigned to one of the two groups. Two participants had accepted the invitation to participate, both allocated to the LF group, but withdrew prior to the intervention period due to a medical condition not related to the intervention ( n  = 1), and personal reasons ( n  = 1). In total, 83 completed the study, 44 in the HF group and 39 in the LF group. No participants experienced any adverse event attributable to the intervention that led to dropout (Figure  2 ).

4

Discussion
Both groups improved knee flexion more than anticipated; however, the between‐group difference was smaller than expected. Both groups demonstrated significant within‐group improvements across secondary outcomes, with no statistically significant between‐group differences.
This study indicates that for individuals with a substantial knee flexion deficit after TKA, a higher daily dosage of self‐managed knee flexion exercise was associated with a greater improvement in flexion compared with a lower dosage. The difference between the groups of 4.1° was less than anticipated, and therefore, the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) warrants discussion. Studies examining the MCID for flexion after TKA demonstrate considerable variability in both definitions and methodological approaches. While Kittelson et al. ( 2020 ) did not explicitly report an MCID value, their findings suggested that changes of ≥ 3° may be clinically relevant when monitoring postoperative recovery. A study on knee flexion among individuals with knee osteoarthritis after non‐surgical intervention showed an MCID for flexion ranging from 3.8° to 6.4° (M. D. Silva et al.  2024 ), highlighting that even relatively small gains in flexion may be meaningful for the individual. This suggests that even a 4° improvement may be clinically meaningful and highlights the need to consider individual functional demands when evaluating improvements in flexion related to activities of daily living. The effect of the intervention on knee flexion remained positive after adjustment for baseline values.
In general, the period of most rapid improvement in knee flexion is observed within the first 4–6 weeks of rehabilitation, followed by a gradual plateau up to 12 weeks and only modest improvements thereafter (Stratford et al.  2010 ; Ebert et al.  2014 ; Kornuijt et al.  2019 ; Kittelson et al.  2020 ). Postoperative rehabilitation guidelines outline that knee flexion typically reaches 100°–110° within 6 weeks and plateaus around 115°–120° by 8–12 weeks after surgery (The Ohio State University,  n.d. ; University Orthopedics and Adult Reconstruction Division,  n.d. ; T. Jakobsen and Mark‐Christensen  2021 ). The participants in this study did not follow the expected postoperative recovery trajectory for adequate knee flexion during the initial weeks of recovery and presented with moderate to severe knee flexion deficits according to the postoperative recovery curve for flexion (Kittelson et al.  2020 ). According to the reference curve, the HF group initiated the intervention 98 days (14 weeks) postoperatively with a flexion of 91°, placing them below the 5th percentile. From this point, they demonstrated a marked improvement in flexion to 105°, ultimately reaching approximately the 17th percentile, with no indication of a plateau during the 18‐day period.
The clinical relevance of reaching 105° of active knee flexion is important, as it directly translates to enhanced performance in daily activities. Walking requires knee flexion between 60° and 90°, depending on ascending/descending slope, which is within the range of baseline flexion observed in the HF group. However, 110° would be a more appropriate goal for stair climbing or the use of chairs without compensations (Rowe et al.  2000 ) and activities such as cycling. Although the participants did not achieve a mean flexion of 110°, their ability to approach this target indicates the potential value of targeted flexion exercise in improving ROM and thereby facilitating everyday activities. Furthermore, the observed increase of 14° in knee flexion over an 18‐day period may serve as a motivational factor for continued exercise, with the potential for further functional and range‐of‐motion improvements.
Due to the limited number of randomized controlled trials, there is little consensus regarding optimal training intensity in rehabilitation protocols. Recommendations emphasize ROM‐focused rehabilitation initiated on postoperative day one and continuing through the first 6 weeks but provide little guidance on training intensity. The rehabilitation program for TKA published by the Capital Region of Denmark (T. Jakobsen and Mark‐Christensen  2021 ) recommends the implementation of exercises targeting ROM during the first 0–6 weeks postoperatively. These exercises can be performed actively or passively, typically at a frequency of 2 × 20 repetitions, twice daily. According to the same protocol, stretching of the knee joint is recommended 2–3 times per week, but these recommendations are based on limited evidence derived from available RCTs. Similar stretching intervals have been reported in other rehabilitation protocols (The Ohio State University,  n.d. ; University Orthopedics and Adult Reconstruction Division,  n.d. ). The lack of strong evidence supporting rehabilitation protocols for mobility training after TKA is noteworthy; therefore, this study suggests that active flexion exercises performed at a high daily frequency in some cases may lead to important improvements in knee flexion among patients with flexion deficits.
On average, the participants in the HF group performed the flexion exercises 5.3 times per day and did not achieve the predefined target of eight daily sessions. Thus, the delivered HF intervention reflected an average moderate rather than a maximal high‐frequency intervention. Consequently, the intervention dose delivered should be interpreted as a study limitation and limits the strength of conclusions regarding a potential dose‐response relationship. In addition, the dose‐response analysis was conducted without adjustments for potential confounders, which may have influenced the observed results. Although adherence was lower than planned, the achieved dose still represents a substantially higher level of daily practice compared with usual care. However, future studies with higher adherence are needed to more precisely examine dose–response effects. Although this deviation limits the ability to fully evaluate a dose–response relationship, it also reflects the realities of routine postoperative rehabilitation and illustrates what may be feasible in standard clinical practice. The level of adherence achieved suggests that the intervention is feasible in a real‐world clinical setting, but that the prescribed exercise frequency may be challenging to sustain outside controlled research conditions. Future trials should therefore explore strategies to support and enhance adherence. In addition, the participants used a logbook, which was found to be a useful monitoring tool and can be recommended for use in clinical practice to support adherence monitoring. During implementation, adherence may be further supported and objectively monitored through digital or technological solutions.
For the secondary outcome knee extension, there was a significant improvement within both groups, but no significant difference between the groups. In the HF group, the extension exercise was performed on average 4.6 times daily, and in the LF group 1.5 times daily, respectively, which in both groups was lower than the average frequency of flexion exercise. This might indicate that the participants did not demonstrate the same level of motivation or perceived need to improve knee extension as compared with knee flexion. For the other secondary outcomes, improvements were observed within the groups, but no difference in changes was found between the two groups. These results are likely attributable to the effectiveness of the basic rehabilitation program that all participants underwent during the 18‐day intervention period. The findings indicate that a basic, group‐based supervised rehabilitation program may lead to improvements in overall functions, pain‐related outcomes, and self‐reported health perception following moderate to severe ROM deficits and a need for intensified rehabilitation after TKA. In addition, it is noteworthy that self‐reported pain levels were generally low, thereby indicating that pain relief had been achieved following TKA. Thus, in this study, pain was unlikely to have been a barrier to achieving greater improvement in knee flexion.
The use of cluster randomization may be viewed as a methodological limitation compared with individual randomization; however, it was necessary due to the hospital's patient admission procedures. On each admission day for the knee cohort, the department receives approximately 22 patients, who are randomly allocated by administrative staff with no knowledge of individual diagnoses. Consequently, the therapists involved in the study had no information regarding which patients were admitted on specific dates. Individual randomization was therefore not feasible, as the risk of contamination between intervention groups would have been unacceptably high. Patients within the same admission cohort interacted closely and were treated by the same clinical team, creating a substantial risk of unintended information exchange, behavioral influence, or shared therapeutic approaches between participants assigned to different study arms. Such contamination effects would have threatened the study's internal validity and compromised the attribution of observed outcomes to the intervention. For these reasons, cluster randomization was deemed the most methodologically appropriate design. Furthermore, as shown in Table  1 , the groups did not differ meaningfully with respect to baseline demographic or clinical characteristics. However, the present use of clusters can introduce an intra‐cluster correlation, which may reduce the independence of observations and affect statistical inference.
A further limitation of the study was the amount of pressure applied to the knee during exercise performance, which was not controlled and likely varied between participants. However, this uncertainty was equally applied to participants in both groups, thereby limiting the risk of differential bias. In addition, the intervention period and follow‐up duration were short‐term, and it would have been valuable to assess the participants at 3 months post‐intervention. However, this was not feasible within the study protocol. As the additional self‐managed exercise intervention is simple to perform, it may hold potential for further long‐term improvements; however, such effects were not assessed or documented in the present study. Furthermore, data on participants' preoperative knee flexion were not collected; therefore, no assumptions regarding preoperative flexion deficits can be made. When comparing the two groups, no statistically significant correlation was observed indicating a dose‐response relationship. The lack of association may be attributable to the absence of baseline measurements incorporated into the study design.
In conclusion, this randomized study demonstrated that individuals with significant flexion deficit 14 weeks after TKA were able to achieve moderate but statistically significant improvements in knee flexion. Although the increase in knee flexion was significantly greater in the high‐frequency exercise group compared with the low‐frequency exercise group, the between‐group difference was smaller than originally hypothesized. Nevertheless, this improvement may still be clinically meaningful for selected individuals with limitations in activities of daily living, where even minor increases in flexion can have a meaningful impact. These findings underline the importance of considering individual needs when implementing rehabilitation protocols.

4.1

Implications on Physiotherapy Practice
In patients who have undergone a total knee arthroplasty and require intensified rehabilitation, a higher frequency of self‐managed flexion exercises is associated with moderate improvements in knee flexion compared with a lower frequency. Range of motion has been shown to increase significantly up to 14 weeks postoperatively for patients following TKA who require intensified rehabilitation.
The use of active exercises can easily be integrated into physiotherapy practice, as it does not require specialized equipment or advanced clinical competencies from the therapist. Given that interventions aimed at improving range of motion are already an integral component of standard clinical rehabilitation protocols, high‐frequency flexion exercises can be easily implemented for patients who deviate from the expected postoperative recovery curve after TKA. Through online consultations, instruction in flexion exercises can be supervised and monitored remotely after the initial introduction. For both inpatients and outpatients, the exercises are easy to perform and generally well tolerated. The exercises can be performed in most environments, require only a limited daily time commitment, and incur no associated cost. Additionally, a digital application may be used by physiotherapists and patients to monitor exercise frequency and adherence.
The study demonstrates clinical relevance by indicating that intensified rehabilitation involving high frequency of exercises can moderately improve knee flexion compared with low frequency. This improvement may reduce the need for further intervention, such as manipulation under anesthesia, if sufficient knee flexion is not achieved within the first 12 postoperative weeks.
Because of the study findings, the internal guidelines at the department where the study was conducted have been revised. For patients with severe knee flexion deficit, the recommended frequency of exercise is eight sessions daily.
